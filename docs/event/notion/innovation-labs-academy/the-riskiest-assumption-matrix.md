# The Riskiest-Assumption Matrix

Count your hypotheses and plot them on two axes: **importance** vs. **evidence**.

- **Important + no evidence** → your riskiest zone. Mark it. You either validate it or knowingly take the risk.
- **Important + evidence** → you're on solid ground.
- If _everything_ is important and you have _no_ evidence for any of it → **don't build yet.** You're working on faith.
  More than half the assumptions in your Lean/Business Model Canvas are probably wrong. Everyone will ask: **"How do you know that? Where's that from?"** The only good answer is: _from talking to users._

---

##### Example: From assumptions to riskiest assumptions

![](images/e281af39acbe.png)

Imagine you're building an AI tool that helps small e-commerce companies answer customer-support emails automatically.

Your initial Lean Canvas might contain assumptions like:

| Hypothesis                                                                           | Importance | Evidence | Risk            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------- | -------- | --------------- |
| Small e-commerce teams receive enough repetitive support requests to need a solution | High       | Low      | 🔴 **Riskiest** |
| Support managers are willing to use AI-generated replies                             | High       | Low      | 🔴 **Riskiest** |
| Companies would pay €100–€300/month for the product                                  | High       | Low      | 🔴 **Riskiest** |
| Shopify stores are the best initial customer segment                                 | Medium     | Low      | 🟠 Risky        |
| Support teams currently use Gmail/Zendesk to manage requests                         | Medium     | High     | 🟢 Lower risk   |
| AI can generate acceptable first-draft responses                                     | High       | Medium   | 🟠 Risky        |
| The product could integrate with existing helpdesk software                          | Medium     | High     | 🟢 Lower risk   |

---

##### Plotting the assumptions

**🔴 Important + No Evidence**

These are the assumptions you should investigate first.

> "Support teams have a painful enough problem that they will actually change their workflow and pay for a solution."

You shouldn't start by building the AI assistant. First, talk to support managers and find out:

- How many support requests do they handle?
- Which requests are repetitive?
- How are they handling them today?
- What is frustrating about the current process?
- Have they tried automation before?
- What would make them willing to pay for a solution?
  **🟢 Important + Evidence**

Suppose you've interviewed 15 support managers and 12 independently describe repetitive customer questions as a major problem.

Now you have evidence supporting the hypothesis:

> "Customer-support teams spend significant time answering repetitive questions."

This assumption moves from **Important + No Evidence** → **Important + Evidence**.

**🟡 Important + Some Evidence**

Suppose 5 of the 15 companies say they would _consider_ paying €100/month.

That's interesting, but it's not strong evidence of willingness to pay

**You now have a new experiment:**

> Ask qualified prospects for a concrete commitment — a paid pilot, pre-order, letter of intent, or similar behavior.

---

##### The key lesson

The matrix isn't asking:

> **"Which assumptions do we think are risky?"**

It's asking:

> **"Which assumptions could kill the business if they're wrong — and what evidence do we actually have?"**

Your customer interviews are therefore not just "research." They are a way of **turning assumptions into evidence**.

The goal isn't to prove that your idea is right.

**The goal is to discover which parts of your business model are wrong before you spend months building them.**
